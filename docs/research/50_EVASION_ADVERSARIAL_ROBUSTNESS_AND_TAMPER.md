# 50 — Evasion, adversarial robustness and camera tamper

*How intruders defeat CCTV analytics, how systems defend, and what SurvSim can
test today. Written 2026-09-26.*

## 0. The one-paragraph answer

There are two very different kinds of "evasion". The first is **the ordinary
kind**: hoods, masks, borrowed hi-vis, crawling, moving slowly, walking in a
crowd, waiting for a PTZ to look away, and simply covering or spraying the
camera. It is cheap, needs no knowledge of the model, transfers to every model
and every human operator, and is overwhelmingly what real intruders do [INF].
The second is **the adversarial-ML kind**: printed patches and clothing
optimised against a specific detector. The published results are real but
narrower than the headlines: a patch can collapse recall of the detector it was
trained against (Thys et al.: YOLOv2 recall 100 → 26.46 % at the clean
operating point), physical T-shirts reach ~50–57 % success against the source
detector, and success decays with viewing angle, distance and change of
detector. The defence that works against both kinds is the same: **don't let
one model be the single point of failure** — multi-camera overlap, a second
modality (thermal, radar, fence), tamper and health monitoring that raises its
own alarm, and evaluation that deliberately red-teams the system with matched
innocent look-alikes so that robustness is not bought with false alarms.

## 1. Threat model: who evades, and what they know

A useful threat model for an intruder detector has three axes [INF]:

| Axis | Low end | High end |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge of the system | none — assumes "there are cameras" | knows the camera positions, PTZ tour, analytic vendor, even model weights |
| Goal | not be *detected* at all | be detected but not *alarmed*; or be alarmed but not *identified* afterwards |

The third axis is the one people forget. A perimeter analytic cares about
*detection* (was there a person in the sterile zone?). A post-event
investigation cares about *identification* (who was it, can we re-find them on
other cameras?). A balaclava does little to defeat a person detector but
destroys face recognition; an adversarial T-shirt may defeat a YOLO detector
but leaves the person perfectly identifiable to a human reviewing the clip.
SurvSim's evasion module encodes exactly this split: each technique records
*which perception cue* it degrades (face, gait, body shape, clothing,
silhouette, height) rather than a single "evades: yes/no" flag [repo:
`survsim/director/evasion.py`, `residual_cues()`].

The white-box adversary (has the weights) is rare in physical security [INF]:
security analytics are typically closed products on closed appliances. But
transfer attacks — optimise against a public detector, hope it transfers — are
cheap, and a vendor that fine-tunes a public YOLO checkpoint inherits some of
its vulnerabilities. That is why transfer limits (§2.4) matter operationally.

## 2. Physical adversarial patches and clothing against person detectors

### 2.1 Thys, Van Ranst & Goedemé 2019 — the patch on a cardboard plate

*"Fooling automated surveillance cameras: adversarial patches to attack person
detection"*, CVPR Workshops (CV-COPS) 2019.
<https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08653> (fetched 2026-09-26).

* First patch attack on a class with high intra-class variety (people), as
  opposed to rigid objects like stop signs (abstract).
* The patch is a printed square held in front of the torso — the
  abstract describes "a small cardboard plate".
* Target: YOLOv2, Inria person dataset. From the PDF body (Fig. 5 / Table 1):
  AP on the Inria test set was CLEAN 100 % (relative to the detector's own
  clean output used as ground truth), random NOISE 94.02 %, and the three
  optimised variants OBJ-CLS 42.8 %, OBJ 25.53 %, CLS 82.08 %. Measured as
  **recall at the clean operating point (threshold 0.4)**: CLEAN 100,
  NOISE 87.14, OBJ-CLS 39.31, **OBJ 26.46**, CLS 77.58.
  <https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.08653> (fetched 2026-09-26).
* The authors framed this operationally: "How many of the alarms generated by a
  surveillance system are circumvented by using our patches?" — the right
  question.
* Stated failure mode: where the patch did not work, "the patch is not aligned
  to the center of the person" (PDF body, §5).

**Read carefully [INF]:** the ground truth is YOLOv2's own clean detections, so
"100 %" is not human-labelled recall; the attack is white-box against one
model; and it was evaluated mostly digitally, with a qualitative physical demo.
The random-noise control (87 % recall) is the important baseline — about a sixth of
the recall drop (13 of 74 points) is just occlusion of the torso by *any* square.

### 2.2 Xu et al. 2020 — the adversarial T-shirt

*"Adversarial T-shirt! Evading Person Detectors in a Physical World"*, Xu,
Zhang, Liu, Fan, Sun, Chen, Chen, Wang, Lin; ECCV 2020 (arXiv Oct 2019).
<https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.11099> (fetched 2026-09-26).

* Models non-rigid cloth deformation with thin-plate-spline (TPS)
  transformations so the pattern survives wrinkles.
* **74 % attack success rate digitally, 57 % physically, against YOLOv2**;
  the abstract contrasts this with 18 % physical success for prior work.

**[INF]** 57 % per-frame success is not 57 % per-intrusion success. A
tracker-plus-rule pipeline that needs the person on *some* frames of a 10-second
approach to raise an alarm will still alarm unless misses are strongly
correlated over time — which they partly are (the same pose and angle persist
for several frames), so the frame-to-event conversion must be measured, not
assumed. This is the single most useful red-team question to ask of any
adversarial-clothing paper.

### 2.3 Wu et al. 2020 — "Making an Invisibility Cloak"

Wu, Lim, Davis, Goldstein; ECCV 2020.
<https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.14667> (fetched 2026-09-26). The value of this
paper is that it is a *systematic transfer study* rather than a single demo.
From the PDF body (<https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.14667>, fetched 2026-09-26):

* Digitally, all learned patches degrade detector AP by at least 29 %, down to
  7.5 % AP when the YOLOv2 patch is tested on a retrained YOLOv2.
* **Transfer is asymmetric**: "Yolo patches can be effectively transferred to
  Faster RCNN models, while Faster RCNN patches are not very effective at
  attacking YOLO models."
* Wearables: 360 photos, ten locations, front/back, two distances.
  "Sweatshirts with Yolov2 patterns achieve ∼50 % success rates, yet they do
  not transfer well to other detectors." Smaller patterns perform worse. FCOS,
  vulnerable to printed posters, "is quite robust with wearable attacks,
  possibly because shirts more closely resemble the clothing that appears in
  the training set."
* Adding TPS non-rigid augmentation "did not improve transferability".
* The paper defines success in three grades — full (no box), partial (box
  covering < 50 % of the person), failure — and reports both AP and success
  rate because "manually selected thresholds are required to compute success
  rates". That is a good protocol template (§7).

### 2.4 Hu et al. 2021 and 2022 — naturalistic patches and multi-angle textures

Two different "Hu" papers are routinely conflated; both matter.

**Hu, Kung, Tan, Chen, Hua, Cheng — "Naturalistic Physical Adversarial Patch
for Object Detectors", ICCV 2021, pp. 7828–7837.**
<https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2021/html/Hu_Naturalistic_Physical_Adversarial_Patch_for_Object_Detectors_ICCV_2021_paper.html>
PDF fetched 2026-09-26 from
<https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2021/papers/Hu_Naturalistic_Physical_Adversarial_Patch_for_Object_Detectors_ICCV_2021_paper.pdf>
(no arXiv version found via the arXiv API title search). Abstract quote below confirmed verbatim.
Table 1 (INRIA, mAP % where clean = 100): their YOLOv2-trained patch leaves
YOLOv2 at **12.06**, versus grey/random controls ~72–75 and the Thys-style
non-naturalistic patch at 2.13 — i.e. naturalness costs ~10 mAP points on the
source detector [values read from a pdftotext table extraction; column
alignment for the other detectors not trusted]. The patch is
searched in the latent space of a pretrained GAN (BigGAN/StyleGAN) so it looks
like a natural image rather than psychedelic noise, trading some attack
strength for inconspicuousness: "significantly more realistic and natural
looking patches than state-of-the-art baselines while achieving competitive
attack performance." **[INF]** This is the version that defeats the *human*
check too — an operator would not look twice at a dog print on a T-shirt.

**Hu, Huang, Zhu, Sun, Zhang, Hu — "Adversarial Texture for Fooling Person
Detectors in the Physical World", CVPR 2022.**
<https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.03373> (fetched 2026-09-26). Toroidal-cropping
expandable generative attack (TC-EGA) produces a *repeating* texture that can
cover arbitrary garments (T-shirts, skirts, dresses). From the PDF body
(<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.03373>, fetched 2026-09-26):

* Metric: mASR = attack success rate averaged over confidence thresholds
  0.1…0.9.
* **Viewing angle**: earlier patch/T-shirt methods "were effective when the
  persons faced the camera … However, the mASRs of these two methods decreased
  when the viewing angle increased." TC-EGA reached mASR ≈ 1.0 at 0° and 180°,
  but was "less effective when the viewing angle is close to 90° or 270°".
* **Distance** (supplementary §E, YOLOv2 T-shirt): mASR **0.791 at 1.6 m,
  falling to 0.257 at 7.0 m**.
* **Transfer** (supplementary §G): YOLOv2 and YOLOv3 clothes transferred to each
  other but "got low mASRs when attacking other models except RetinaNet";
  Faster/Mask R-CNN clothes transferred better.

### 2.5 What the transfer limits mean for a surveillance deployment [INF]

1. **Distance is the defender's friend.** Every physical result above is at
   1.5–7 m. Perimeter and CNI cameras typically image people at 20–100 m where
   the person is 30–150 px tall; a pattern that needs fine texture detail to
   steer the network is low-pass-filtered away by optics, compression and
   `degrade.py`-style blur. The 0.79 → 0.26 decay over 1.6 → 7 m is consistent
   with that. Nobody has published a working attack at CCTV standoff that I
   found.
2. **Angle diversity is the second friend.** Overlapping cameras view the same
   person from different azimuths; a texture that fails near 90° fails on at
   least one camera in a well-designed rig.
3. **Detector diversity is the third.** Cross-family transfer is weak (YOLO ⇄
   R-CNN asymmetric). Running two architectures, or a detector plus a
   motion/background-subtraction channel that has no gradients to attack, forces
   the attacker to beat both.
4. **Thermal ignores printed colour.** A visible-band pattern does nothing to a
   thermal camera — but see §4.2: thermal has its own physical attacks.
5. **Patches are a real threat to *automated, unattended* single-model
   pipelines** — e.g., occupancy counting, automated access control, drone
   detection — more than to a verified alarm pipeline with a human in the loop.

### 2.6 Defences against patches

*"Revisiting Adversarial Patch Defenses on Object Detectors: Unified
Evaluation, Large-Scale Dataset, and New Insights"* — Zheng et al., ICCV 2025.
<https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.00649> (fetched 2026-09-26):

* Benchmark of 2 attack goals, 13 patch attacks, 11 detectors, 4 metrics;
  a dataset of 94 patch types and 94,000 images.
* Naturalistic patches are hard to defend because of **data distribution, not
  high-frequency content** — defences that look for high-frequency noise miss
  them. Training on their diverse patch set improved existing defences by
  ~15 %.
* **Adaptive attacks substantially circumvent current defences**; defences with
  complex/stochastic models or that exploit universal patch properties hold up
  better.
* The AP of the targeted objects correlates better with defence effectiveness
  than patch-detection accuracy does — i.e. evaluate the end task, not whether
  you spotted the patch.

**[INF] Practical defence order for a CCTV product:** (1) architecture —
multi-view, multi-modal, non-learned motion channel; (2) adversarial training
with diverse naturalistic patches; (3) patch/anomaly detectors as a secondary
signal; (4) evaluate against *adaptive* attacks, not only the published patch.

## 3. Ordinary evasion: camouflage, timing, crowds and pretexts

### 3.1 Camouflage and low contrast

Dark clothing against dark backgrounds, or clothing matched to vegetation,
reduces the foreground/background contrast that both background-subtraction
and learned detectors rely on [INF]. At night the question moves to the
illuminator: under IR illumination, fabric reflectance in the near-IR, not its
visible colour, sets contrast — some dark fabrics are bright in NIR and some
light ones are dark [INF; consistent with standard NIR imaging practice, not
fetched here]. SurvSim's `degrade.py` night profiles model the camera side of
this, but not per-fabric NIR reflectance [repo; see 30_SYNTHETIC_DATA §CCTV
render chain for the gap].

### 3.2 Thermal suppression and spoofing

*"Fooling thermal infrared pedestrian detectors in real world using small
bulbs"* — Zhu, Li, Li, Wang, Hu; AAAI 2021.
<https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.08154> (fetched 2026-09-26):

* Digital patch: YOLOv3 AP dropped by **64.12 %**; physical board of small
  bulbs: AP dropped by **34.48 %**.
* Controls: a blank board already caused **29.69 % (digital) and 14.91 %
  (physical)** AP reduction — i.e. roughly half the physical effect is just
  occlusion by a board.
* Reported "good transferability" to unseen detectors.

**[INF]** Low-tech thermal suppression — insulating layers, umbrellas, emergency
blankets — attacks the same cue, and like the blank board, most of the effect
comes from occluding the warm torso. A red team should always run the *blank*
control before crediting "adversarial" optimisation.

### 3.3 Timing attacks

* **Slow movement.** Many motion-based detectors and legacy VMD have a minimum
  velocity or a frame-difference threshold; a person moving very slowly across
  the field can stay below it [INF]. Learned per-frame detectors are not
  velocity-gated, but trackers and rules often are (e.g. a "running" or
  "direction" rule). The i-LIDS sterile-zone scenarios included deliberately
  varied approach styles for this reason (see 28 §i-LIDS).
* **Crawling / crouching.** Detectors trained on upright pedestrians (COCO,
  CrowdHuman) under-represent prone and crawling postures [INF]; the silhouette
  aspect ratio changes, and height-based filters ("ignore objects < 0.8 m
  tall" used to suppress animals) actively reject crawlers — a false-alarm
  suppression that becomes a miss mode [INF].
* **Tailgating / piggybacking.** Not a detector attack but a *rule* attack: the
  authorised person generates the valid access event and the second person
  shares it. Commercial analytics now ship dedicated rules — Axis lists a
  "Tailgating Detection" scenario in AXIS Object Analytics
  (<https://www.axis.com/products/axis-object-analytics>, fetched 2026-09-26).
* **PTZ timing.** Moving while a patrolling PTZ looks elsewhere. Only works if
  the tour is predictable; randomised tours and fixed overview cameras defeat
  it [INF].
* **Blind-spot changes.** Changing clothes where no camera sees defeats
  appearance ReID across cameras; it does not defeat gait or
  spatio-temporal priors [INF].

### 3.4 Crowd hiding

In a dense crowd, occlusion reduces detection recall and ID switches rise, and
appearance ReID has more distractors [INF; see 26 MOT deep dive for MOT20
density figures]. The attacker's aim is usually not to avoid detection
(everyone is detected as "a person") but to avoid *being singled out* and to
break track continuity. The defender's counter is not better detection but
better association: multi-camera geometry and time-of-flight priors.

### 3.5 Pretexts (role disguise)

Hi-vis vest, clipboard, ladder, courier uniform. These defeat **humans and
rules**, not detectors: the person is detected perfectly, but the behaviour
("working at height near the fence") looks legitimate. This is where evasion and
hard negatives meet: real maintenance workers vastly outnumber pretexters, so
an analytic that flags hi-vis is useless [INF]. SurvSim encodes this with a
`benign_rate` — for `hi_vis_worker` it is 800× more common innocently than
hostilely [repo].

## 4. Camera tamper and sensor spoofing

### 4.1 Tamper classes

The standard taxonomy is **occlusion (cover), defocus, displacement (move/
redirect)**, plus in practice spray/obscuration, glare/dazzle, and signal loss.
SurvSim's camera director implements six kinds —
`("cover", "spray", "tilt", "unplug", "glare", "defocus")` [repo:
`survsim/director/camera_director.py` line 20] — and principle 2 in CLAUDE.md
requires ground truth to follow the live (tampered) camera.

### 4.2 Classical detection

**Ribnick, Atev, Masoud, Papanikolopoulos, Voyles — "Real-Time Detection of
Camera Tampering", AVSS 2006.** Located via search 2026-09-26
(<https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Real-Time-Detection-of-Camera-Tampering-Ribnick-Atev/83051896c30d55062be0e5aa4ec1bf97cfe83748>).
Compares short-term and long-term image buffers using edge-orientation
histograms and related features to detect occlusion, defocus and displacement.
IEEE AVSS 2006, doi:10.1109/AVSS.2006.94 (Crossref). **Body now fetched**
(open copy linked by Semantic Scholar, <http://people.zoy.org/~sam/4020669.pdf>,
2026-09-26): three dissimilarity measures (chroma histogram, L1 histogram,
gradient-direction histogram) between an older and a recent frame buffer,
~3 fps. Table 1, 20 real tampering events: at the optimal thresholds **5 false
alarms and 1 missed event (95 % events detected, 79.2 % of alarms true)**;
4 of the 5 false alarms were lights being switched off. The earlier
secondary figure "3.1 % missing / 7.8 % false alarms" does **not** appear in
the paper — **corrected**.

**[INF] What classical detectors measure**, and their characteristic false
alarms:

| Tamper | Feature | Classic false alarm |
|---|---|---|
| Cover | loss of edges/entropy, histogram collapse to dark | night falling, lights switched off, a lorry parked in front |
| Spray/obscure | global loss of high-frequency energy | rain on the dome, fog, condensation |
| Defocus | drop in gradient magnitude / Laplacian variance | fog, heavy compression at low bitrate |
| Move/tilt | background-model mismatch, feature-point displacement | legit PTZ move (must be suppressed via ONVIF PTZ state), wind shake |
| Glare/dazzle | saturated area, blown histogram | low sun, headlights, IR reflection off a nearby wall |
| Unplug | no stream | network outage — indistinguishable at the VMS |

### 4.3 Recent and learned approaches

**Mantini & Shah — "A Survey of Feature Types and Their Contributions for
Camera Tampering Detection"** (arXiv Oct 2023, already in FACTS as "Tamper
survey 2023"). <https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07886> (fetched 2026-09-26). Casts
tamper detection as change detection over a time series, computes ten feature
types from real surveillance footage, and evaluates their predictability with
time-series models.

**Ma, Yan, Wu — "Clean-Reference Streaming Detection of Lens Occlusion and
Photometric Transitions for Camera Tamper Monitoring"**, arXiv 2607.14760
(submitted 16 Jul 2026). <https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14760> (fetched
2026-09-26). Luminance/gradient statistics against a clean reference, with
structured-light rejection and rapid-brightness suppression. Reported: F1 0.800
and balanced accuracy 0.822 on 320 controlled sequences; **0.925 recall at
0.025 false-positive rate** in stress testing (a threshold sweep that reuses
the generated data — the authors call it "an FPR-constrained sensitivity
diagnostic rather than a held-out operating point"); on UHCTD 0.667 in-scope recall
vs 0.016 out-of-scope recall; and **zero false alarms across 9.09
camera-hours** in a verified-negative audit.

**[INF] Reading those numbers the way doc 49 teaches:** zero false alarms in
9.09 camera-hours gives, by the rule of three, a 95 % upper bound of ~0.33
false alarms per camera-hour — far above an ARC budget. To claim < 0.01/cam-h
you need ~300 clean camera-hours; that is exactly the kind of long, rendered-
free negative run the mock backend can generate cheaply. The 0.016
out-of-scope recall is also honest and useful: the method detects the tamper
types it was built for and essentially none of the others — so a product needs
a *family* of detectors, one per tamper class.

Learned approaches (CNN classifiers over frame pairs, trained on synthetic
tamper) exist; the recurring problem is that synthetic tamper is easy to
generate digitally (blur, black box, shift) and the classifier learns the
synthetic artefact [INF]. That is a sim2real question SurvSim can address
because tamper is rendered in-scene (a physical cover, a real tilt) rather than
pasted in post.

### 4.4 Sensor spoofing: IR floods, lasers and dazzle

* **Laser.** *"Adversarial Laser Beam: Effective Physical-World Attack to DNNs
  in a Blink"* — Duan et al., CVPR 2021. <https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06504>
  (fetched 2026-09-26). Tunes a laser beam's physical parameters (wavelength,
  width, angle, position) so a classifier mispredicts; demonstrated digitally
  and physically. It attacks classifiers, not person detectors in a video
  pipeline — a gap, not a demonstrated CCTV attack [INF].
* **IR flood / dazzle.** A bright NIR source saturates an IR-sensitive camera at
  night; to a human it is invisible. The camera's AGC pulls exposure down and
  everything else goes black [INF]. Detected as glare tamper if the tamper
  detector watches for saturated area; SurvSim's `glare` kind is the analogue
  [repo].
* **Thermal bulbs** (§3.2) spoof a thermal detector with a physical heat
  pattern.

**[INF] Defence pattern:** treat every blinding event as an *alarm in its own
right* (the camera is now blind — that is evidence of intent), and cross-check
with another modality that the spoof doesn't reach (radar is unaffected by
light; a visible camera is unaffected by a NIR flood if it has an IR-cut
filter in day mode).

## 5. Why red-teaming must include innocent look-alikes

A robustness improvement that raises false alarms is not an improvement. The
matched-pair principle in `evasion.py` — every hostile persona has a benign twin
wearing exactly the same things — and `audit_confounds()`, which checks that no
attribute leaks the label and that protected attributes (niqab, burqa, hijab,
turban, wheelchair) are balanced, are there so that an evasion-robust model
cannot cheat by learning "hood ⇒ intruder" [repo]. The module docstring puts it
as "an attribute is not an intent".

Operationally [INF]: if you "fix" the hood-up recall gap by training on more
hoods-up intruders, you will also raise the alarm rate on every hood-up
commuter in the rain. Measure both, at the operator budget (doc 19 §6.2:
0.1 FA/min = 6 FA per camera-hour; doc 49 on how many camera-hours you need to
bound it).

## 7. Anti-evasion evaluation protocol


1. **Define the unit of success at the event level**, not the frame: "was an
   alarm raised within the i-LIDS tolerance for this intrusion?" Frame-level
   ASR (Thys, Xu, Hu) is an input, not the answer.
2. **Always include controls**: blank/random patch of the same size (Thys noise
   87 % recall; Zhu blank board −14.91 % AP), plain clothes of the same colour,
   and the clean run. Credit only the delta over control.
3. **Sweep the nuisance axes the papers show matter**: distance (Hu 2022:
   0.79 → 0.26 over 1.6–7 m), viewing angle (weak at 90°/270°), detector family
   (asymmetric transfer), illumination, compression bitrate, and camera height.
4. **White-box, transfer and adaptive**: test the published patch, a patch
   optimised on a public proxy, and an attacker who knows your defence (Zheng
   2025: adaptive attacks break most defences).
5. **Pair every attack with its innocent twin** (evasion.py matched pairs) and
   report the false-alarm cost of any robustness gain in FA/camera-hour at the
   operator budget.
6. **Report with intervals** (doc 49): binomial CIs on per-event success,
   rule-of-three bounds on zero-FA runs, enough seeds.
7. **Report per-camera and per-rig**: an attack that beats one camera but not
   the overlapping one is a rig-level *success for the defender*.
8. **Tamper**: per tamper class, time-to-detect and nuisance tamper alarms per
   camera-hour under weather/light change — the confusers in §4.2's table.

---


**Q1. How would you red-team an intruder detector?**
Start from the threat model, not from adversarial ML. List what a real intruder
does — cover or spray the camera, approach slowly or crawl, use a blind spot or
the PTZ tour, wear dark clothes at night, use a pretext like hi-vis. Build a
scenario matrix over those techniques × distance × angle × light × weather,
each paired with an innocent twin. Score event-level detection within the
i-LIDS tolerance and false alarms per camera-hour. Only then add adversarial
patches, with blank-patch controls and transfer across detector families. The
deliverable is a ranked list of *system* weaknesses, not one ASR number.

**Q2. The adversarial T-shirt gets 57 % success. Should a perimeter customer
worry?**
Some, but read the conditions: 57 % per frame, against YOLOv2, the model it was
trained on, at a few metres (Xu 2020). Wu 2020 found similar ~50 % shirts
"do not transfer well to other detectors"; Hu 2022 measured the YOLOv2 T-shirt
falling from 0.79 to 0.26 mASR between 1.6 and 7 m. At perimeter standoff, with
overlapping cameras and a thermal or radar channel, the attack has to beat
several independent sensors on every frame of a long approach. I'd test it
rather than dismiss it — especially for unattended automated uses.

**Q3. How do you detect camera tamper without drowning the ARC in nuisance
alarms?**
Per-class detectors (cover, defocus, move, glare, signal loss), each compared
against a slowly updated clean reference, with explicit suppression for known
confusers — scheduled lighting changes, PTZ moves reported over ONVIF, weather.
Require persistence (seconds, not a frame). Measure nuisance tamper alarms per
camera-hour on long negative runs; a 9-camera-hour zero-FA result (Ma 2026)
only bounds the rate below ~0.33/cam-h, so you need hundreds of hours.

**Q4. Someone crawls across the sterile zone. Why might you miss it?**
Detectors trained on upright pedestrians under-represent prone postures; the
box aspect ratio changes; and the height/size filter you added to reject foxes
now rejects the crawler. Fix by including crawling in training/eval and by
filtering on track behaviour (persistence, direction into the zone) rather than
height alone — and measure the fox false-alarm rate after the change.

**Q5. How do you stop robustness training turning into profiling?**
Matched pairs: every hood-up intruder has a hood-up commuter; every hi-vis
pretexter has a real worker. Audit that no attribute predicts the label and that
protected attributes (faith coverings, mobility aids) are balanced — SurvSim's
`audit_confounds()` does this. Intent has to come from behaviour over time in
context, never appearance.

**Q6. What's the cheapest effective attack on a CCTV analytic, and the cheapest
defence?**
Covering or spraying the lens, or just unplugging it. The defence is to treat
loss of view as an alarm — a camera that goes blind at 03:00 is itself a strong
intrusion indicator — plus overlap so one blinded camera doesn't create a blind
zone. `survsim rig` can show which zones lose coverage when any single camera
is removed.

**Q7. How would you evaluate an adversarial-patch defence properly?**
End-task metrics (AP / event recall of the protected person), not "did I detect
the patch" — Zheng et al. 2025 found target AP correlates better with defence
quality. Include naturalistic patches (they aren't high-frequency), and adaptive
attacks that know the defence. And report the defence's false-alarm cost on
clean footage.

**Q8. What can SurvSim test today, and what can't it?**
Today: rules, tracking and ReID continuity, PTZ timing, blind-spot changes, crowd
cover, tamper events with ground truth that follows the live camera, and the
false-alarm cost of every change, cheaply over hundreds of camera-hours in the
mock backend. Not yet: pixel-level wardrobe evasion (masks, coats, adversarial
textures) and gait spoofing — blocked on the MetaHuman wardrobe/gait route —
and any thermal attack, since there is no thermal sensor. I'd say that
explicitly rather than let a cue-level number stand in for a pixel-level one.
